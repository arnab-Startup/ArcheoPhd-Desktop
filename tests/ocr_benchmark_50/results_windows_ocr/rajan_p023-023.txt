Antiquity of Mankind The development of prehistoric archaeology depended on the emergence of an evolutionary perspective in geology and biology particularly in palaeontology, In the first half of the 19th century geologists, biologists and archaeologists encountered human physical remains and stone tools associated with the bones of e.xtinct animals in stratified deposits in many parts of the Europe. It was [he French custom inspector Boucher de Perthes working in the gravel quarries of Somme river in 1841 who published a convincing evidence for the association of human artefacts (prehistoric tools) and the bones of extinct animals. With this discovery, the biblical notion of the creation of the world in 4004 BC was questioned and led to a widely agreed belief that human origins extended far back into the remote past. The outstanding publications of English Geologist Charles Lyell's (1797-1875) Principles of Geology, Charles Darwins's On lhe Origin of Species published in 1859 and Herbert Spencer's ( 1820-1903) evolutionary approach to scientific and philosophical problems revolutionised the thinking of the contemporary scientific community. According to Darwin, in the struggle for existence, environmentally better-adapted individuals of a particular species would survive whereas the less well-adapted ones would die. The surviving individuals would pass their advantageous traits by heredity to their offspring and gradually the characteristics of a species would change to such an extent that a new species emerged. In Darwin's words, the underlining essential mechanism was natural selection or the survival of the fittest. Darwin's concept of natural selection was accepted by many scientists and members of the public as providing mechanism that made it possible to believe that a process of biological evolution accounted for modern species and explained the changes observed in the palaeontological record. The obvious implication is that man is evolved from ape-like primate. This made the antiquity of human species a burning issue and prehistoric (palaeolithic) archaeology acquired a high-profile role in the study of human mankind. Prehistoric Archaeology The term prehistory itself came into use after the publication of John Lubbock's book Prehistoric Times in 1865. He divided the Stone Age into Old Stone Age (palaeolithic) and New Stone (Neolithic) based on the tools, which are chipped in former and grounded and polished in the latter. Influenced by the evolutionary theory, archaeologists started looking into the stone tools and the associated fossil remains that are found in different geological context to understand the evolution of man and tried to place them in a chronological order. The Scandinavian archaeologists were involved in identifying the cultural evolution of the man whereas the French and British archaeologists devoted much of their time in identifying the antiquity of mankind. This approach could be seen in Edouard Lartet (1801-71) and Gabriel de Mortillet works. Palaeolithic archaeology, however, aroused great public interest because it revealed the hitherto unexpected antiquity of humanity. It also set new standards for stratigraphic analysis in archaeology. Palaeolithic archaeology enjoyed great prestige because of its close ties with geology and palaeontology. Different stages of human evolution were established. A distinctive cultural evolution was framed. Racism One of the main problems that had confronted cultural evolutionism from the beginning was to explain why some societies had developed rapidly while others apparently had remained static over thousands of years.
