Underwater Archaeology This branch of study is also known as nautical archaeology and marine archaeology. Presently underwater archaeology concentrates more on the shipwrecks and submerged ports and cities] The goals of underwater archaeology are often historical in orientation. The shipwreck is considered as time capsule as they provide an important clue on the trade and technology of the specific period. The subject matter of this recent discipline extends to studies of ancient trade, commerce, ship building technology, navigation, etc. The discovery and retrieving of King Henry VIII's Mary Rose and the Titanic are considered as one of the remarkable achievements in the field of underwater archaeology. The discovery of Dwaraka off Gujarat coast and the celebrated early historic port at Kaveripattinam by the National Institute of Oceanography under the direction of S.R.Rao are some of the important works undertaken in India. t It is an interdisciplinary study' undertaken by archaeologists with the active co-operation of oceanographers and geophysicists with a view to reconstruct the history of shipbuilding, overseas trade and interactions of cultures. At the same time, it generates data of scientific and educational value in respect of eustatic studies and coastal erosion. The effect of environment on perishable and non-perishable material is better understood by a study of datable finds from submerged archaeological sites and from shipwrecks. i. Ethnoarchaeology Archaeologists in recent decades have developed a new branch of study called ethnoarchaeology. Ethnoarchaeology is the study of living peoples and of their material culture undertaken with the Introduction 1 5 aim . of improving our understanding of the past. Like ethnographers, archaeologists are used to live among contemporary communities with specific purpose of understanding their material culture like how they make stone tools, build houses, do agriculture and so on. For instance, the study undertaken by Lewis Binford by living among the Nunamiut Eskimo of Alaska gave him way to new ideas in interpreting archaeological data collected from that region. The study of traditional bead making centres of Khambat in Gujarat and Kangayam in Tamil Nadu yielded a useful data on understanding the technology involved in making of various beads that we recovered from archaeological sites.' j. Linguistic Archaeology Linguistic archaeology is the study of language and script in the backdrop of archaeological remains to identify cultural identities like the race, migration, ecology, philosophy and religion. The epigraphical records found on metal, stone and manuscripts are studied in order to understand the material remains of a particular region. For instance, in India, painted grey ware is associated with Aryans and megalithic monuments are associated with Dravidians. Recently, the cultural materials that are found on the banks of river Saraswathi are associated with Aryans. Guntur Kossinna connected the corded ware with Indo-European language, which later led to identify Germany as the homeland of Indo-European language. This identification led to the emergence of pure race theory, which was totally exploited by Hitler. In the modern world, giving a linguistic affinity to a particular community and identifying the archaeological remains with that particular people are still doing in the corner. k.
