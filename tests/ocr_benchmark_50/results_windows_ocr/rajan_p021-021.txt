It was in the y•ear 1849 that Henry Rawilson succeeded in deciphering the long trilingual text that the Achaemenid king Darius I had carved on cliff at Bisitun (also Behiston or Bisotun) in Iran. This decipherment provided the means to unravel the history of ancient Babylonia and Assyria and an outline chronology of Mesopotamian civilization was established. The progress in the study of Egyptology and Assyriology became manifold with these decipherments. Such progress also was noticed in China during Qing dynasty and in Japan during Tokugawa period. However, India did not witness such a situation. It was only during the Colonial period that the interest on the monuments was developed. Irrespective of the great discoveries and decipherments, the text-aided archaeology did not allow the scholars to push the age of the earth beyond biblical year of 4004 BC. Even the artefacts and monuments were associated with certain rulers or with their ancestors. For instance, Geoffrey of Monmouth stressed England's earlier Britis, rather than its Anglo-Saxon past in order to please his Norman masters. He associated Stonehenge with Arthurian legend. German megalithic graves were often associated with Huns, who invaded Europe in the 5th century AD. Even prehistoric remains were also associated like this. However, this trend could not continue longer. The decline of feudalism, the development of national states, growth of patriotism and progress in science induced the people to examine the antiquities, artefacts and monuments beyond the biblical age. Antiquarianism Antiquarianism is the study of antiquities in orderly manner to establish its age and function with specific scientific input of that time. Systematic antiquarian research developed in Scandinavia rather than in other parts of Europe, as part of political rivalry that followed the separation of Sweden and Denmark in 1523. Johan Bure, Swedish civil servant and Ole Worm, a Danish medical doctor, respectively encouraged by Gustavus II Adolphus of Sweden (161 1-1632) and by King Christian IV of Denmark (1588-1658) started documenting large monuments with late Iron Age inscriptions. This guided the archaeologists to focus on I-lislory of Archaeology 23 prehistoric period from classical period and in turn, led to the establishment of museums and Royal collections. National Laws were passed to protect ancient monuments. The surrender of valuable finds to the king in return for a reward was encouraged through these laws. Unfortunately, antiquarian research was decelerated due to the slowdown of the economy in both Sweden and Denmark by the end of 1 7th century. The attention to the prehistoric monuments was revived in the 1 8th century with the identification of monuments associated with Celtic inhabitants (the Indo-European language speaking people). In the later part of 18th century, a growing interest to demonstrate the cultural achievements of the Celts, who -were recognised as the ancestors of the French, encouraged the study of pre-Roman times to develop independently of classical archaeology. Such developments also emerged in other parts of the Europe. The nationalism fever encouraged more fanciful speculation than sober investigations. In the absence of written records, no system was developed to date the prehistoric artefacts anywhere in Europe. "Ihs ultimately contributed little to the development of archaeology. The European colonisation in the 17th century led to the contact of the people in other parts of the world. They came across the people who had lived in non-metallic world. Descriptions of these people and their customs circulated in Europe created some awareness about different societies and helped to realise that the stone tools found in Europe had been manufactured by human beings. Scholars also reaiised that stone tools were probably used before metal. The recognition of pre-metal age helped to establish prehistory later. They also started or inclined to believe that the technologically less advanced peoples thtt they were encountering in various parts of the world as survivals of a primordial human condition rather than as products of degeneration. However, without adequate chronological controls and lack of understanding of the evolutionary view of human history, strong religious faith or sanctions made many antiquarians reluctant to challenge it. Irrespective of this, the realisation of stone age and metal age paved a way for the development of three-age theory.
