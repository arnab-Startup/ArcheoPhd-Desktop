Chapter 3 History of Archaeology in India The ancient civilization of India differs from those of Egypt, Mesopotamia and Greece. Unlike other ancient civilizations, India has preserved its tradition without a break down to the present day. The visible monuments like stupas, temples, mosques, forts, rock-cut caves, pillars and other architectural edifices embedded with numerous inscriptions in diftérent languages and scripts were always objects of reverence. However, there is hardly any concrete and cohesive attempt to expose the Indian history and culture in toto. The main reason for this situation is that except the history of the kings of Kashmir written by Kalhana in the 12th century, there is no proper historical chronicle in India. The great books like Rigveda. Mahabharata, Ramayana. Puranas and others could not yield any reliable historical events. Even the inscriptions are not uniformly distributed both in time and space. There are more inscriptions in the south than in the north. Until recently, it is believed that Indians lacked a sense of history and branded as an ahistorical people. The Indian scholars like Pargiter, D.D.Kosambi, R.C.Majumdar, R.S.Sharma, Romila Thapar and others were the first to attempt a comprehensive critical review of this point of view. They have undertaken a detailed examination of the historiographical value of ancient writings that were embedded in the Puranas, vatnsavalis and prasastis. Indian rulers also tried to understand and preserve their cultural heritage. Some of the rulers made persistent efforts in collecting and preserving the cultural material of the past. The Chola king Rajaraja I tried to make anthology of the Tevaram hymns. The Maratha Sarafoji collected valuable manuscripts, which are preserved in the Saraswathi Mahal at Thanjavur. The royal collections preserved in the Salarjung Museum, Hyderabad, Ramnagar Palace Museum, Varanasi and Swai Man Singh Museum at Jaipur are similar ones. Firuz Shah Tughlak (1351-1388) removed two inscribed Asokan pillars one from Meerut and another from Ambala to his capital Delhi with the curiosity to understand the information found History of Archaeology in India 3 1 engraved on these pillars. However, the rediscovery of India's past was mostly due to European effort. Europeans made an earnest attempt to understand the various facets of Indian culture so as to rule their subjects effectively. Foundation for the Indological Studies Early Notices The Colonial Europeans, on their first visit to India, found a culture fully conscious of its own antiquity and not changed tundamentally for many thousands of years. Britain claims a major share in its discovery and is followed by Germany, France, Portuguese, Dutch and other countries. Europe learnt about the existence of Sanskrit from the correspondence of the first Jesuit in India, St Francis Xavier (1506-1552), who arrived in Goa in 1542. In a letter written in 1544, he quoted the Sanskrit invocation Om Srii naraina nama and translated into Latin. This was the first Sanskrit text known to Europe. It did not take long for the European Classical languages trained .Jesuits to discover their kinship with Sanskrit. Fr Thomas Stephens (author of the first Konkani grammar) and Fillipo Sasseti (an Italian merchant in Goa) described the relations between Latin and Sanskrit in their writings. Fr Roberto de Nobili (1577-1655), Brahmanised Jesuit from Italy who came to Madurai in 1606, is acknowledged as the first European Sanskrit scholar. Abraham Roger, a chaplain at the Dutch settlement of Pulicat in south India from 1631 to 1641 translated some of the proverbs of poet Bhartrhari into Dutch and thereby introduced the Sanskrit work to Europe. The early Jesuit, French and British etTorts were sporadic and mostly out of personal curiosity. They made no real attempt to study India's past. and her early history was known only from brief passages in the works of Latin and Greek authors. They did not attempt to understand the historical background of the culture, rather they tried to link the Indians with the descendants of Noah and the vanished empires of the Bible. Only a few devoted missionaries in the Peninsula gained a deep understanding of contemporary Indian life through a brilliant mastery of the vernaculars. Only in the later part of the 1 8th century, the foundation for the Indological studies was laid on firm ground.
