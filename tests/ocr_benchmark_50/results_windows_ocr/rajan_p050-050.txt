It involves the removal of the superimposed layers one by one in reverse order to the way they were laid down, gradually revealing each successive stage in the history of the site. It must be conducted methodically so that whatever is found can be seen and studied within its own context. It is necessary to maintain a careful record in order to understand the stratigraphical position of objects and architectural features. So, archaeological excavation might be defined as the disciplined disassembling of the contexts, which form a site with the aim of explaining the origin of each layer and its relationship to the rest of the site. In other words, excavation is the removal of the matrix in order to discover and retrieve archaeological data from beneath the ground, thereby revealing the three dimensional structure of the data and matrix both vertically and horizontally. An archaeological excavation is usually complicated and painstaking process. There are many reasons for carrying out archaeological excavations and many ways to determine the appropriate techniques to apply in the field. In this chapter a wide range of excavation techniques are reviewed. Some are largely outdated and some have modified to suit the present need and become part of archaeology's history. It is very difficult to demarcate any specific technique as the sites are formed in ecological, geological and geographical settings. The success of the archaeological excavation remains with the archaeologist who has a flexibility and open-mindedness in his approach. As Sir Mortimer Wheeler (1956:81) said "The experienced excavator, who thinks before digs, succeeds in reaching his objective in a majority of cases".
