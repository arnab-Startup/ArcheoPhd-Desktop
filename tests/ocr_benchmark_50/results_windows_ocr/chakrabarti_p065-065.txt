In the Buddhistical worship of trees displayed in the Sanchi bas-reliefs, others, I hope, will see (as well as myself) the counterpart of the Druidical and adopted English reverence for the oak. In the horse-shoe ternples of Ajanta and Sanchi many will recognize the form of the inner colonnade at Stonehenge. More, I suspect, will learn that there are cromlechs in India as well as in Britain; that the Brahmans, Buddhists and Druids all believed in the transmigration of the soul; that the Celtic language was undoubtedly derived from the Sanskrit; and that Buddha (or wisdom), the Supreme Being worshipped by the Buddhists, is probably (most possibly) the same as the great god Buddwas, considered by the Welsh as the dispenser ofthe good. These coincidences are too numerous and too striking to be accidental. The megalithic origin of the Buddhist stupas was written about by Stuart Piggott in the 1940s, and in Cunningham's apparent belief in the eastern origin of the Druids on finds a throwback to the earlier ideas of Reuben Burrow, T. Maurice and others. We have noted elsewhere that the idea of a relationship between the Buddhists and the Druids persisted till the middle of 24 the nineteenth century. Even in this work Cunningham appealed to the Court of Directors of the East India Canpany for a government-sponsored archaeological survey: As the opaling of the Bhilsa topes has produced such valuable results, it is much to be hoped that the Court ofDirectors will, with their usual liberality, authorize the employment of a competent officer to open the numerous topes which still exist in north and south Bahar, and to draw up a report on all the Buddhist remains of Kepila and Kusinagara, of Vaisali and Rajagriha, which were the principal scenes ofSakya's labour. A work of this kind would be of more real value for the ancient history of India (the territory ofthe Great Company) than the most critical and elaborate edition 25 of the eighteen Puranas. The Bhilsa Topes is a very fine publication of Alexander Cunningham. In its drawings of the ground plans of the excavated stupas and the sculptured reliefs of the Sanchi gateways and the careful description of the work done in the light of the history of Buddhism, the volume amply conveys the first flush of pleasure in the presence of something as beautiful as the early Buddhist remains ofSanchi and its neighbourhood.
