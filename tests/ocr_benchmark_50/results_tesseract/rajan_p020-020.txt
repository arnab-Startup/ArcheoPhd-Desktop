Classical Archaeology

The appreciation of classical antiquity took a momentum in late
14" century AD. Cyriacus of Ancona (Ciriaco de’ Pizzicolli), who
travelled in Greece and Mediterranean for 25 years for
documenting the archaeological monuments, collecting books and
documents and copying the inscriptions, is considered to be as the

first archaeologist. Unfortunately, his six volumes of
commentaries on inscriptions were destroyed in a fire in 1514. For
a long time, there was hardly any excavation in the modern sense
but merely digging in search of objects. The excavations of
Roman site at Herculaneum and Pompeii, lying at the foot of
Mount Vesuvius in the Bay of Naples, Italy are of that sort only.

The landowner under which Pompeii was buried due to the
eruption of nearby Vesuvius in August 79 AD, rented the right to
entrepreneurs to dig there-by cubic yard. It evoked great interest in
classical antiquity among the nobles. This interest gradually
spread throughout the Europe. The members of royal families
started collecting antiquities through different agents. For
instance, the Duke of Buckingham Charles I and the Earl of
Arundel were rivals in importing such art works during 1
century. This way, antiquities started accumulating in private
hands. The accumulated classical art, objects were placed for
investigation. This led to the establishment of art history as a
separate branch. German scholar Johann Winckelmann’s work
Geschichte der kunst des Altertums (History of Ancient Art 1764)
provided the first periodization of classical art. This Classical
study provided a model for the development of Egyptology and
Assyriology.

The people did not know the ancient civilisations of Egypt
and the Near East until late 18" century, except what had been
recorded about them in the Bible and by the ancient Greeks and
Romans. The systematic investigation of ancient Egypt began with
observations made by the French scholars who accompanied
Napoleon Bonaparte’s invasion of Egypt in 1798-9. They
produced the multi-volume Description de I'Egypte in 1809. It
was during this French occupation, the bilingual inscription
engraved with Greek, Demotic and Hieroglyphic scripts on a black
basalt stone was discovered by one of the officers of Napoleon in
1799 placed as part of fort at Rashid near Alexandria. This world
famous Rosette stone presently housed in the British Museum
helped to crack the ancient Egyptian scripts by Jean-Francois
Champollion in 1822. At the same time circus performer Giovanni
Belzoni and the agents of the French Consul-General Bernardino
Drovetti plundered the Egyptian tombs and temples to display in
France and Britain. This plundering however was halted by French
Egyptologist Aguste Mariette in 1858 after getting appointment as
a Conservator of Egyptian Monuments.
