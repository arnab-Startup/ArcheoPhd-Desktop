Elephanta and (presumably) Kanheri received fuller treatment at the
hands of another Portuguese, Dom Joao de Castro who came to India in 1538
and was the viceroy of Goa during 1545-48. His attitude was one of unabashed
admiration. Regarding Elephanta he writes:

All the works, images, columns, reliels, workrooms, which are there are
carved in the massive stone of the mountain, all of which seem to pass
beyond the bounds of nature; indeed the proportions and the symmetry with
which each figure and everything else is made would be well worth the while
ofl'any painter to study, even if he were Apelles.?’

There is an attempt to convey the plan of the complex along with some
measurements, and as Mitter correctly notes, it is possible from his description
to identify at least three iconographic types of Siva in the sculptures.?

He was no less explicit on the “pagode of Salsette” or Kanheri.

In the whole of this building there is not a single image, column, house,
portico, figure, pillar, cistern, temple, chapel or anything else which is not
carved out of the very stone of this rock, a thing which certainly is not within
the capability of mortals. Through many investigations, I believe this work
to be so amazing that it is almost one of the seven wonders of the world . . %

In this case also de Castro offers an understanding of the plan and measure-
ments.

Mitter points out that by the middle of the sixteenth century information
about Elephanta had reached the west through the Jesuitsources. Ina volume
published in 1575 in Paris on universal cosmography the author, A. Thevet,
not only referred to the “incredible grandeur” of Elephanta but also “sought
to trace the imprint of Classical Antiquity in the art of Elephanta.”? It has also
been pointed out by Mitter that in an Italian publication of 1590 Elephanta
was considered an antique Roman temple, supposedly built by Alexander to
mark the end of his military advance in India.?®

In contrast to observations such as these the late sixteenth century observa-
tions of Linschoten on Kanheri and Elephanta, which were modelled on those
of Garcia da Orta, appear to be fairly bland.
