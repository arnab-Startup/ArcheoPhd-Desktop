The Beginning of a Systematic Scholarly Interest

The turning point was the middle of the eighteenth century when there were
two important lines of development. First, accurate and precise descriptions of
monuments began to appear, and secondly, research began primarily on the
historical geography of the country. Anquetil du Perron who is introduced on
the title page of his book Zend-Avesta, Ouvrage de Zoroastre® as the royal interpre-
ter of oriental languages and as belonging to the Académie Royale des Inscrip-
tions and Belles-Lettres came to India in 1754. In 1758 he minutely examined
the rock-cut caves of Ellora and measured their dimensions. He was interested
in the plans of the complex as well as in the associated mythology.® In about
1760 du Perron investigated Elephanta:

My first attention to the pagodas of Elephanta was to look for ancient
inscriptions as at Keneri. I found only the names of travellers chiefly English
and Portuguese; I contented myself therefore with taking the dimensions of
the excavations and proportions as at Keneri.>'

The plate IV of the first volume of his book shows the plans of Salset and
Bassein, Djegueseri caves, Monpeser or Mandapeswar caves, caves of
Elephanta and Kanheri and the inscriptions of Kanheri*? After surveying
Ellora du Perron wrote:

India is a fruitful country which will offer always to the real scholar, and
even to a person of common curiosity, a plentiful harvest of objects of
research equally useful and interesting. I have gleaned at a time of life when
one’s strength is not always equal to one’s spirit, and at a time when the fury
of war had ravaged the finest provinces.”

Carsten Niebuhr, a captain of engineers in the service of the king of
Denmark, came to Bombay from Mokha in Arabia on 13 September 1763. The
second volume of his Voyage en Arabie and en d’autres Pays Circonvoisins published
in Amsterdam in 1780 contains nine illustrations of Elephanta.®* An abridged
English translation of his work was published in Edinburgh in 1792. Niebuhr
was possibly the first to concentrate almost exclusively on Elephanta. The
section 30, chapter 6 of the second volume of his work contains his observa-
tions on this site.

Several travellers mention the isle of Elephanta, and the Indian temple in
it; but thisisonly in a transient manner, and without seeming to have known
all the importance of those remains of remote antiquity. To me the temple
appeared so remarkable, that I visited the island three different times, in
order to draw, and describe its curiosities.
